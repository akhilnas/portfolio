_This is work I did for my employer. I've left out client names and production figures and generalised some details, so this page covers the design and the reasoning rather than the results._

## Background

My team ran a separate deep learning model for each NLP task, including classification, named entity recognition, information retrieval and relation extraction. Each one was trained, deployed and maintained on its own.

Llama 2's release made it plausible to collapse that set into a single model. The company also held substantial proprietary medical datasets, so the question was whether those datasets could specialise one open-weights LLM onto the tasks the separate models were doing.

## My Role

I was the sole developer and researcher on this project. The model was used in a pilot and is no longer in use: newer LLMs now give sufficient performance out of the box.

## Key Decisions

### LoRA and quantisation, because of the hardware

Full fine-tuning of a 13B model doesn't fit on one NVIDIA A100. Using a LoRA adapter with 4-bit quantisation is what made the largest model the hardware allowed trainable at all.

### Spot instances, with resumability as a precondition

Each run took 41 hours on a rented A100 spot instance. A job that long on a preemptible instance will meet a reclaim sooner or later, so resuming had to work before spot was an option at all. Checkpoints were written every few training steps, and no interruption ever cost a run.

### Version the generated data

Generated instruction datasets were versioned with DVC in buckets, rather than left as loose outputs of the generation loop.

## How It Was Evaluated

I compared the same model before and after fine-tuning on held-out proprietary test sets for classification and multi-label classification, and on the anatomy and clinical knowledge subsets of MMLU, chosen as adjacent to the medical domain.

Fine-tuning improved on the base model in every comparison. Multi-label classification gained the most, and the other gains were modest.

## The Outcome

Consolidating models always had a cost: it trades individually tuned, strong small models for one model to serve, version and maintain. To be worth that, the general model had to match them.

It didn't. On tasks that already had a dedicated model, the task-specific models still outperformed the fine-tuned Llama 2, so we didn't consolidate and the specialised models stayed in production. The organisation moved to general-purpose LLMs later, once off-the-shelf models were strong enough to do the work without fine-tuning.

## Lessons Learned

- **Compare against what's already in production.** Beating the base model was the easy test. The one that decided the outcome was beating the incumbents.
- **Make long jobs resumable before you need them to be.** At 41 hours a run, checkpointing was what made cheap hardware usable.
- **Hardware limits can set the design.** The choice of model and training method followed directly from what fits on one GPU.
- **A negative result is still an answer.** The evaluation showed that consolidation wasn't worth it yet.
