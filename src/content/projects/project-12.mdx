## Background

German electricity is priced one hour at a time in a daily auction, and the next day's prices can swing from well above €100/MWh in the evening to below zero at midday when solar floods the grid. Forecasting those prices is a well-worn machine learning problem, and it's easy to stop once the error metric looks good.

I wanted to answer a more practical question: what is a better forecast actually worth in money? And, once that was answered, could someone like me trade on it?

## Project Scope

To build the whole path from raw market data to a scheduled forecast, then hold the result to an economic test. Every model is compared against a simple baseline in euros, and a model only replaces the current one if it clearly earns it.

The target is the DE_LU bidding zone, which covers Germany and Luxembourg. The project also grew two side tracks: spread forecasts against France and the Netherlands, and an attempt at natural gas futures.

A FastAPI service and a Streamlit dashboard read only the stored results and never call the model directly, so a failed run leaves the last good forecast on screen. A weekly champion/challenger job reports whether a retrained model beats the current one, and promotion needs a manual step and at least a 2% improvement. An optional stage writes a market report through Amazon Bedrock, but it isn't part of the results here.

**Deployment status:** the scheduled ECS tasks, EventBridge rules and the rest of the AWS stack are defined in Terraform and pass validation, but I haven't provisioned them. Everything below ran locally on real ENTSO-E data.

## Results

### Grid data cut the error by 12%

Adding day-ahead load, wind and solar forecasts took validation RMSE from 47.66 to 41.97 €/MWh on the same 252 training days and 63 validation days. Residual load became the top feature by gain, more than ten times ahead of the runner-up. That fits how the market works: what's left for conventional plants to cover largely sets the price.

### A better forecast is worth a modest amount over naive

The backtest runs a simulated 1 MWh / 0.5 MW battery with 88% round-trip efficiency from 1 May to 10 July 2026, before fees and degradation. Each day it charges and discharges on the forecast, and the naive baseline simply reuses the previous day's prices.

| Schedule | Profit (71 days) | Capture ratio |
|---|---|---|
| Perfect foresight | €11,562 | 1.000 |
| **GridCast forecast** | **€11,454** | **0.991** |
| Naive (yesterday's prices) | €10,983 | 0.950 |

The capture ratio is the share of perfect-foresight profit a schedule achieves. The model's training data ends on 8 May, so 8 of the 71 days overlap with it. On the 63 held-out days alone, the result is 0.990 against 0.948, almost the same.

This is the number I find most useful: the forecast adds about €472 over naive, or roughly €6.60 per day per MWh of battery. Yesterday's prices already capture 95% of the value, because the daily shape of solar and demand repeats. The model's value is in that last 4%.

![A forecast for 12 July 2026 with its 80% interval, following the shape of actual prices but dropping far below the actual midday low](/portfolio/gridcast-forecast.png)

The chart shows why a 0.99 capture ratio can sit next to a €42 RMSE. On 12 July the model forecast a midday low of about −€80 when the actual low was much closer to zero, a large miss. It still found the right hours to charge and discharge, and that ordering is what a battery schedule depends on. Daily rank correlation between forecast and actual prices averaged 0.97.

### Spread trades look promising, but the evidence is thin

A paper strategy trading 1 MWh per hour on the sign of the forecast price difference was profitable on both pairs:

| Pair | Profit | Capture ratio | Hit rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germany − France | +€2,084 | 0.49 | 66% |
| Germany − Netherlands | +€1,234 | 0.55 | 65% |

The window is only 12 days (1 to 12 July 2026), and the French and Dutch models had just 27 training days. It's a reason to keep looking, not a finding.

## The Catch: A Retail Trader Can't Trade This

A forecast being worth money doesn't mean I can collect it, and in Germany this turned out to be the biggest obstacle of the project.

- **The auction isn't a retail venue.** EPEX SPOT's day-ahead market is wholesale, and its members are companies such as producers, suppliers, large consumers, aggregators and trading firms. I didn't find a broker that lets an individual trade tomorrow's hourly prices.
- **Direct access is an institutional setup.** It takes a company, exchange admission with KYC checks and a trader exam, clearing through ECC, balancing arrangements as a Balance Responsible Party (or through a market access provider), and REMIT registration with the Bundesnetzagentur.
- **Power futures don't express this signal.** EEX Phelix-DE futures are the professional instrument, and one month of baseload at 1 MW is about 720 MWh, or roughly €72,000 of notional at €100/MWh. They also settle on average prices over a month or longer, while this model's edge is in the hour-by-hour shape of the next day.
- **CFDs are the nearest retail product, and still not the same thing.** Saxo launched a CFD on EEX German power futures for private investors in 2018. I couldn't confirm whether it's still offered, and it tracks futures rather than the next day's auction.
- **Gas and carbon futures capture only part of the price.** TTF gas and EU carbon futures are more accessible and they influence power prices, but they miss the weather and renewables signal this model is best at.

The one legitimate way for an individual to settle at day-ahead prices is as a consumer. German suppliers have had to offer dynamic tariffs since 2025, so a home battery or EV can charge in the cheap hours, which is the same strategy as the backtest. It's also a very different scale: a 10 kWh battery cycling daily on an €80/MWh spread earns roughly €200 to €350 a year.

So every result on this page is a paper result, and this is why the gas futures track below exists: it was an attempt at a market a retail trader can actually reach.

## What Didn't Earn Its Place

- **Natural gas futures: no significant edge.** I pointed the same discipline at CME Henry Hub gas, which is available through mainstream futures brokers. A walk-forward backtest over 2018 to 2026 gave a 51.8% hit rate (z = 0.69) and a Sharpe ratio of −0.09 (z = −0.26), both indistinguishable from no edge, and it lagged buy-and-hold on risk-adjusted measures. My verdict was not to trade it, and not even to build a paper-trading loop on this result.
- **The quantile model wasn't promoted.** A quantile XGBoost candidate (v0.4.0) was meant to improve the prediction intervals. It had worse pinball loss and worse coverage than a plain residual-based interval in every configuration I tried, so v0.3.0 stayed in production.
- **Conformal intervals are sharper but under-cover.** Conformal prediction gave the lowest pinball loss, but reached only 0.56 to 0.67 coverage in testing against a target of 0.80, while the residual-based method reached 0.83 to 0.89. Its rolling window lags when volatility jumps. It's wired in and takes over once enough residuals accumulate, but with no live production history, I can't yet claim its coverage is right.

## Lessons Learned

- **Judge a forecast by the decision it drives.** RMSE alone would have hidden both how much the model helps and how little that help is worth over a trivial baseline.
- **Check market access first.** I'd have saved time by asking who is allowed to trade this before asking how to predict it.
- **Write the promotion rule before seeing the result.** A pre-agreed bar made it straightforward not to ship a model I'd just spent time building.
- **Test significance before believing a backtest.** The gas results looked like a strategy until the z-scores showed they couldn't be told apart from chance.
- **Power market data has sharp edges.** Delivery days run on Central European time rather than UTC, daylight saving changes the number of hours in a day, and ENTSO-E revises published values, so the pipeline re-fetches recent days and every stage is safe to re-run.
