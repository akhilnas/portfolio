## Background

We were ingesting large-scale real-world data from hospitals into an oncology document-processing programme, and pages and documents needed classifying by content. The first approach was supervised, and it needed large-scale human annotation that didn't carry over to proprietary datasets.

The limit was annotation capacity rather than model quality, so a better classifier wouldn't have helped. We needed to change what humans were asked to label.

## My Role

I led development end to end on this internal tool. Work from this project was filed as a US patent.

## Method

The idea is that documents of the same kind tend to share a structure, so the structure can identify the class without anyone defining the classes first.

## Key Decisions

### Label pairs, not classes

Instead of annotating documents against a class list, a person looks at a small sample of pairs and judges each one similar or dissimilar. These labels are the only supervision, and they place the decision boundaries. That's why I call the method semi-supervised rather than unsupervised: it still uses human judgement, but it changes what the human has to judge.

Answering "are these two documents the same kind of thing?" takes no class taxonomy and no agreement on what the classes are. The supervised approach needed a fixed class list and annotation at corpus scale before any of it could run.

### Let the corpus decide how many classes exist

Pairwise judgements never commit to a class count, and template discovery doesn't need one either. The number of document classes comes from the corpus rather than from a preset list.

### Represent a document by its headers

Headers carry a document's structural identity, which is what separates one template from another, and they keep the representation small.

## How It Was Evaluated

The evaluation follows Marina Meilă, _Comparing Clusterings: An Axiomatic View_ (ICML 2005), which axiomatically characterises criteria for comparing partitions. F1 is computed over pair decisions from the similar/dissimilar judgements, with the Mirkin metric used to compare partitions.

The Mirkin metric counts the pairs that are co-clustered in one partition but not the other. Because it's a pair-counting measure, pairwise labels are its natural ground truth, and the evaluation and the labelling method fit together.

## Limits

- By construction, two documents with the same headers but different body content look almost identical under this representation. It tells you which template a document follows, not what it says.
- The method still needs human judgement, in a much smaller and simpler form.

## Lessons Learned

- **Find the real bottleneck.** The constraint was annotation capacity, and that pointed to a different kind of labelling rather than a different model.
- **Ask what the label needs to contain.** A pair judgement carried enough supervision for this problem without requiring a taxonomy.
- **Choose metrics that match your labels.** A pair-counting metric made a small set of pairwise judgements directly usable for evaluation.
